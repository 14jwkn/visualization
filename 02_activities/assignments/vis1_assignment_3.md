# Visualization 1

### Dataset: https://open.toronto.ca/dataset/major-crime-indicators/

- For each visualization, describe and justify: 
    > What software did you use to create your data visualization?

    I used Python, specifically the matplotlib and seaborn packages.

    > Who is your intended audience? 

    Residents, journalists, and policymakers interested in public safety trends, specifically major crime, in Toronto.
    
    > What information or message are you trying to convey with your visualization? 

    This line chart shows yearly counts of major crime categories, highlighting trends over time. The goal is to inform viewers about which types of crime are increasing (e.g., Assault) or stable (e.g., Robbery) over time and their relative frequencies, helping communities and policymakers understand public safety patterns.
    
    > What aspects of design did you consider when making your visualization? How did you apply them? With what elements of your plots? 

    I employed aspects of design based on the module materials. The line chart was designed to balance aesthetic appeal, accuracy, and perceptual clarity. Color and layout choices were made to maximize visual clarity and reduce extraneous cognitive load. Each crime category is represented by a distinct color to make trends distinguishable without overwhelming the viewer. The lines are thick to be clearly visible, and the title is left-aligned to guide the eye naturally from top-left, following reading patterns. Temporal data is displayed along a linear x-axis with yearly ticks to ensure that viewers can accurately perceive trends over time. This supports substantive accuracy, as viewers can trust the visualization to represent yearly fluctuations without approximation. The legend is positioned outside the plotting area and vertically centered to prevent obstruction of data, reducing extraneous cognitive load and supporting rational appeal, by presenting the data transparently. A source annotation is included at the bottom-right in bold gray text, reinforcing reproducibility and moral appeal. The visualization is perceptually intuitive. Line charts are a familiar format for representing trends, helping the general public quickly grasp changes across crime types. These choices support emotional and moral engagement, as viewers can contextualize trends in public safety.
    
    > How did you ensure that your data visualizations are reproducible? If the tool you used to make your data visualization is not reproducible, how will this impact your data visualization? 

    The source data is cited and a Python script was written - the plot can be reproduced by downloading the data and running the code.
    
    > How did you ensure that your data visualization is accessible?  

    I used a color palette with high contrast discrete colors designed for colorblindness, adjusted sizing for readability, and made lines thicker to improve contrast.
    
    > Who are the individuals and communities who might be impacted by your visualization?  

    Residents, community safety organizations, policymakers allocating resources for policing or intervention, journalists reporting crime trends, etc.
    
    > How did you choose which features of your chosen dataset to include or exclude from your visualization? 

    I only included the year and crime type to make the visualization simple to understand.
    
    > What ‘underwater labour’ contributed to your final data visualization product?

    This includes data collection by Toronto Police Services and Open Data teams, community input informing crime categories and reporting standards, technical work cleaning data, developers supporting Python visualization tools, etc.
